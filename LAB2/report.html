1. Short theory

A monoalphabetic cipher replaces every occurrence of a letter with the same substitute throughout the message. Its key is an arbitrary permutation of the alphabet, so the key space is 26! ≈ 4.03 × 1026 — far too large to search. The cipher is nevertheless broken routinely, because the key space is not where its weakness lies.

The weakness is that the substitution leaves the structure of the language untouched. Each plaintext letter maps to one ciphertext letter and always the same one, so everything that makes a letter recognisable survives into the ciphertext: how often it occurs, which letters it sits next to, which short words it forms, whether it doubles. The attacker does not need the key — the text carries enough of the language to give the key away.

SignalWhat it gives away
Letter frequenciesE is the most common letter of English at 12.70%, then T, A, O, I, N
Short wordsThe commonest three-letter word is THE; two-letter words are mostly OF, TO, IN, IS, AS
BigramsTH, HE, AN, IN, ER, ON, RE, ED, ND, HA, AT, EN
TrigramsTHE, AND, THA, ENT, ION, TIO, FOR, NDE
Doubled lettersSS, EE, TT, OO and FF are the frequent ones

The attack works by hypothesis and confirmation rather than by calculation. The frequency order of the cryptogram is lined up against that of the language, which suggests a few substitutions; those are made, and the partly decrypted text is read to see which words are beginning to appear. A word that becomes readable fixes several more letters at once, and each new letter makes more words readable. The process converges quickly and is self-checking — a wrong guess produces unpronounceable text and is abandoned.

Two conditions have to hold: the message must be long enough for its letter frequencies to resemble those of the language, and the language of the plaintext must be known. Both are satisfied here.

4. Breaking the cipher

The attack proceeds exactly as in section 2.3 of the assignment. After every deduction the substitutions found so far are applied and the result is read back. Letters already recovered appear in lowercase; letters still unknown stay as the UPPERCASE ciphertext letter, so a half-broken word such as tIe shows at a glance what is missing.

The running key is written as 26 characters — the plaintext letter that each ciphertext letter a to z stands for, with a dot where nothing has been decided yet. Only the opening of the message is reproduced at each step; python3 main.py walkthrough prints all eight stages in full.

5. The key and the message

Putting the two alphabets side by side gives the key, in the form of table 2.5 of the assignment:

ciphertext   a b c d e f g h i j k l m n o p q r s t u v w x y z
plaintext    b q f u j y n c r g v k z o d s h w l a p e t i x m

Read in the other direction — what each plaintext letter was enciphered to — the key is:

plaintext    a b c d e f g h i j k l m n o p q r s t u v w x y z
ciphertext   t a h o v c j q x e l s z g n u b i p w d k r y f m

A check on the answer

The second line is not a random permutation. Writing each letter as its position in the alphabet, the encipherment is

c = (7x + 19) mod 26

an affine cipher: a (0) goes to 19 = t, b (1) goes to 26 mod 26 = 0 = a, c (2) goes to 33 mod 26 = 7 = h, and so on for all 26 letters.

Nothing in the attack relied on this — the letters were recovered one at a time from frequencies and words, which is what the task asked for. But it is a strong confirmation that the solution is correct: a key that was even slightly wrong would show no such structure, so the odds of stumbling onto a clean arithmetic rule by accident are negligible.

Showing a half-broken message

def partial_decrypt(text, key):
    out = []
    for char in text:
        if not char.isalpha():
            out.append(char)
            continue
        lowered = char.lower()
        if lowered in key:
            out.append(key[lowered])
        else:
            out.append(lowered.upper())
    return "".join(out)

This is what produces the intermediate texts in section 4. A letter already recovered is written in lowercase, a letter still unknown is left as the uppercase ciphertext letter, and anything that is not a letter is passed through untouched — which is what keeps the word boundaries and punctuation visible while the message is only half broken.

Repetition patterns

def pattern(word):
    seen = {}
    out = []
    for letter in word:
        if letter not in seen:
            seen[letter] = ALPHABET[len(seen)].upper()
        out.append(seen[letter])
    return "".join(out)

A substitution cannot change the shape of a word, so pattern("wqvw") and pattern("that") are both ABCA. Comparing patterns is what narrows a cipher word down to a handful of candidates, and it is the reason XZUNPPXAXSXWF could only be IMPOSSIBILITY.

Recovering the key automatically

The manual attack was also checked by a program that knows nothing about the message. It starts from the key that matches the two frequency orders — the opening move of the manual attack — and then improves it by swapping pairs of letters:

def _climb(key, scorer, rng):
    """Swap plaintext letters until no single swap improves the score."""
    best = dict(key)
    best_score = scorer.score(best)
    letters = list(ALPHABET)
    improved = True
    while improved:
        improved = False
        pairs = [(a, b) for i, a in enumerate(letters) for b in letters[i + 1:]]
        rng.shuffle(pairs)
        for first, second in pairs:
            candidate = dict(best)
            candidate[first], candidate[second] = best[second], best[first]
            candidate_score = scorer.score(candidate)
            if candidate_score > best_score:
                best, best_score = candidate, candidate_score
                improved = True
    return best, best_score

The score decides what counts as progress, and it combines two signals because neither is enough alone:

for word in self.words:

Letter-pair statistics give a usable gradient while the text is still gibberish, when no real words have appeared yet; counting words of the dictionary sharpens the last steps, weighted by the square of the word length because a long word matching is far stronger evidence than a short one. Hill climbing can still stall in a local maximum, so the search restarts from shuffled keys and keeps the best result.

Run on this cryptogram the program returns bqfujyncrgvkzodshwlapetixm — the same key recovered by hand, and the main confirmation that the manual attack did not go wrong anywhere.

Checks

Seventeen automated checks cover the pieces: that the key is a one-to-one mapping of all 26 letters, that only letters are substituted and capitalisation is preserved, that every step of the walkthrough only adds letters and that the eight steps end at the recovered key, and that the plaintext contains the phrases it should. Two of them are worth singling out:

  • the solver is given the recovered plaintext re-enciphered with a random key and has to break that too, which tests the method rather than the answer;
  • the key is checked against the affine rule c = (7x + 19) mod 26 for all 26 letters.

7. Conclusions

The cipher has 26! ≈ 4.03 × 1026 possible keys and was broken in eight steps, by hand, without trying a single one of them. That is the whole lesson of the laboratory work: the size of the key space says almost nothing about the strength of a cipher. What matters is whether the ciphertext still carries the structure of the language, and a monoalphabetic substitution carries nearly all of it.

The attack was not really a search. Each step was a hypothesis that could be read back and checked immediately — if WQV had not been THE, the next line would have been nonsense and the guess abandoned at no cost. Because the evidence accumulates, the work gets easier as it goes: the first deduction needed the frequency table, and the last seven letters fell out of single words in which they were the only unknown.

Two details were worth noticing in practice. First, the frequency table is a way in, not an answer: lining up the two orders rank for rank was correct for only 9 of the 26 letters. It was correct for E, T, A and O, and that was enough to make the text readable; everything after that came from words, not counts. Second, what gave the message away fastest was not the letters at all but what was left around them — the word boundaries, the punctuation and the capitalisation. WQV standing alone 43 times is a much louder signal than any frequency, and a cipher that stripped the spaces would have been appreciably harder work.

The practical conclusion is that a monoalphabetic cipher is unusable for anything real. Hiding the word boundaries, padding the text or enciphering in fixed-length groups would slow an attacker down but would not fix the underlying flaw, since one plaintext letter still always produces the same ciphertext letter. The flaw is only repaired by making the substitution change as the message goes on — which is what polyalphabetic ciphers do, and which is the next thing to study.
