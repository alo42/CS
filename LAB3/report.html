1. Short theory

A monoalphabetic cipher replaces every occurrence of a plaintext letter with one and the same ciphertext letter. That single property is what broke the cipher of Laboratory Work No. 2: the substitution is fixed, so the frequency profile of the language passes through into the cryptogram unchanged and the key can be recovered by counting letters, without ever searching the key space.

A polyalphabetic cipher removes exactly that property. The substitution applied to a letter is no longer constant — it depends on where the letter stands in the text. If the letter T is sometimes enciphered as a and sometimes as b, and X is also sometimes enciphered as a and sometimes as b, then the high frequency of T is blended with the low frequency of X and the resulting distribution is flatter than either. The simplest illustration uses two alphabets alternately, one for the even positions and one for the odd ones, for instance the two permutations

p1(a) = (3·a) mod 26   and   p2(a) = (7·a + 13) mod 26.

The first polyalphabetic system is credited to Leon Battista Alberti, and the method that carries the name of Blaise de Vigenère was in fact published earlier, in 1553, by Giovan Battista Bellaso; Vigenère himself described a stronger, related cipher in 1586. The name stuck to the simpler construction anyway.

The Vigenère cipher

The Vigenère cipher is a Caesar cipher whose shift changes at every position. The key is not one number but a sequence of shifts — in practice a word, so that it can be memorised — which is repeated from the beginning as many times as the text requires. Writing mi, ci and ki for the numeric codes of the i-th letter of the plaintext, of the cryptogram and of the key stream, and n for the size of the alphabet:

ci = (mi + ki) mod n   and   mi = (ci − ki) mod n

with ki = key[i mod |key|]. For the Romanian alphabet n = 31 and the letters are coded A = 0, Ă = 1, Â = 2, …, Z = 30. As in Laboratory Work No. 1, the subtraction is written as (ci − ki + n) mod n, because in several languages the remainder of a negative number is itself negative.

The same transformation can be carried out without arithmetic, using the Tabula Recta — the square in which row k holds the alphabet rotated by k positions. To encipher, one looks up the row of the key letter and the column of the plaintext letter; the cell where they meet is the ciphertext letter. The table is only a different presentation of (m + k) mod n, and the program prints it for the 31 Romanian letters.

Why the cipher is stronger — and where it still fails

Two things improve over the monoalphabetic case. The first is the key space. The assignment requires a key of at least 7 letters, and over 31 letters that already gives

317 = 27 512 614 111 ≈ 2.75 × 1010

keys of that length alone — against the 30 keys of the Caesar cipher of Laboratory Work No. 1 — and the attacker does not know the length either, so the lengths above 7 must be searched as well. The twelve-letter key used in the runs below is one of 3112 ≈ 7.9 × 1017.

The second, and the more important one, is that the frequency profile of the language no longer survives. A plaintext letter is enciphered through |key| different Caesar alphabets depending on its position, so one plaintext letter produces several different ciphertext letters and one ciphertext letter stands for several different plaintext letters. Section 3 shows both effects happening inside a single 36-letter message.

The cipher is nevertheless breakable, and the attack is classical. Because the key repeats, two identical groups of letters in the plaintext that happen to fall on the same offset of the key produce two identical groups in the cryptogram; the distance between such repetitions is a multiple of the key length. Collecting those distances and taking their common divisors — the Kasiski examination — reveals |key|, which the index of coincidence (the Friedman test) estimates statistically as well. Once the length is known the cryptogram is split into |key| columns, each of which was enciphered with a single shift, and every column is then broken by the frequency analysis of Laboratory Work No. 2. The cipher is therefore secure only while the key is long and never reused — in the limit, a key as long as the message and used once is the one-time pad, which is provably unbreakable.

A letter's numeric code is its index in this tuple, which is what the assignment means by "codificate cu numerele 0, 1, … 30". No ord() or chr() appears anywhere in the cipher, so the diacritics are not a special case: Ă, Â, Î, Ș and Ț are letters at positions 1, 2, 11, 22 and 24, and the wrap-around from Z back to A needs no extra handling.

The assignment states the permitted characters as "între 'A' și 'Z', 'a' și 'z'" while also asking for the 31-letter Romanian alphabet; the two are reconciled by reading the range as the Romanian A … Z, diacritics included, which is the alphabet the message is written in. Lowercase input is accepted and uppercased, and Ş/Ţ typed with a cedilla are folded onto Ș/Ț with a comma below, since both spellings circulate on keyboards.

Preparing the text

The assignment prescribes the preparation exactly: "în mesaj mai întâi trebuie eliminate spațiile, apoi toate literele se vor transforma în majuscule". One function does both and rejects everything else:

def normalize_text(raw):
    text = unicodedata.normalize("NFC", raw)

    letters = []
    for position, char in enumerate(text, start=1):
        if char.isspace():          # spaces are allowed in the input...
            continue                # ...and simply dropped
        char = CEDILLA_VARIANTS.get(char, char)
        char = LOWER_TO_UPPER.get(char, char)
        if char not in LETTER_TO_CODE:
            raise InvalidCharacterError(char, position)
        letters.append(char)
    return "".join(letters)

The exception carries the offending character and its position, so the message printed to the user names what was rejected, where it was, and what the permitted range is — the "i se va sugera diapazonul corect al caracterelor" of the assignment.

The key

The key is validated through the same function, with one additional rule — the length:

MIN_KEY_LENGTH = 7       # "Lungimea cheii nu trebuie sa fie mai mica de 7"


def parse_key(raw):
    try:
        key = normalize_text(raw)
    except InvalidCharacterError as error:
        raise InvalidKeyError(
            "the key may contain only letters of the Romanian alphabet: %s"
            % error
        )
    if len(key) < MIN_KEY_LENGTH:
        raise InvalidKeyError(
            "the key %r has %d letter(s); it must have at least %d"
            % (key, len(key), MIN_KEY_LENGTH)
        )
    return key

Because the key passes through normalize_text first, spaces in it are removed before the length is measured, so c h e i a a is six letters and is rejected.

Encryption and decryption

The key stream is the key repeated for as long as the text needs it, and the two ciphers are the two formulas of section 1, applied letter by letter:

def key_stream(key, length):
    """Repeat `key` until it covers `length` letters: k_i = key[i mod |key|]."""
    return "".join(key[position % len(key)] for position in range(length))


def encrypt(text, key, alphabet=ALPHABET):
    """c_i = (m_i + k_i) mod n, letter by letter."""
    to_code = _codes(alphabet)
    n = len(alphabet)
    stream = key_stream(key, len(text))
    return "".join(
        alphabet[(to_code[letter] + to_code[k]) % n]
        for letter, k in zip(text, stream)
    )


def decrypt(text, key, alphabet=ALPHABET):
    """m_i = (c_i - k_i) mod n, letter by letter."""
    to_code = _codes(alphabet)
    n = len(alphabet)
    stream = key_stream(key, len(text))
    return "".join(
        alphabet[(to_code[letter] - to_code[k] + n) % n]
        for letter, k in zip(text, stream)
    )

The ordered alphabet is a parameter rather than a constant. That is what makes it possible to run the very same functions over the 26 letters of English and compare the output against the example printed in the assignment, before trusting the code on 31 letters — the check is in section 3.

Two further functions exist only to make the cipher visible. steps() returns the six rows of the table used in the assignment to work the cipher by hand — the input letter, the key letter, their codes, the result of the modular addition and the output letter — and tabula_recta() builds the 31 × 31 square described in section 1.

The polyalphabetic effect

This single 36-letter message is already enough to show what the cipher is for. The plaintext letter A occurs seven times and, depending on the key letter standing over it, comes out as six different letters; conversely, the ciphertext letter I stands for three different plaintext letters in the same message:

Plaintext letterAAAAAA
Key letterAETRIC
BecomesAETRIC
Ciphertext letterIII
Key letterCRI
Stands forESA

A monoalphabetic cipher can do neither. This is precisely the property that makes the letter-counting attack of Laboratory Work No. 2 useless here: the frequency of A in Romanian is spread over six ciphertext letters, and the count of I in the cryptogram is a mixture of the counts of three unrelated plaintext letters.

4. Conclusions

The Vigenère cipher fixes the one defect that destroyed the ciphers of the first two laboratory works, and it does so with almost no extra machinery: the code of this implementation differs from the Caesar cipher of Laboratory Work No. 1 in a single detail — the shift is read from a repeating key stream instead of being a constant. That one change is enough to break the link between a plaintext letter and a ciphertext letter. The runs in section 3 show it concretely: in a message of 36 letters, one plaintext A already produces six different ciphertext letters, and one ciphertext I covers three different plaintext letters. The frequency count that broke the cryptogram of Laboratory Work No. 2 returns nothing useful here.

The cipher is still not secure, and it is worth being precise about why, because the reason is no longer the one from the previous works. It is not the key space: with keys of 7 letters or more over 31 letters, exhaustive search is already out of the question. The weakness is the repetition of the key. The cryptogram is in truth |key| Caesar ciphertexts interleaved, so an attacker who recovers the key length — by the Kasiski examination on repeated groups, or by the index of coincidence — reduces the problem to |key| independent monoalphabetic ciphers and solves each of them with the method of Laboratory Work No. 2. The longer the key relative to the message, the fewer letters fall in each column and the harder that last step becomes, which is why the assignment sets a minimum length and why the limiting case — a random key as long as the message, used once — is the only unbreakable cipher there is.

On the implementation side the lesson of Laboratory Work No. 1 held again and paid off twice. Keeping the alphabet as explicit ordered data, with a letter's code being its position in that list, meant that the 31 Romanian letters needed no special treatment at all — a plain letter enciphering to Ț is simply what (6 + 18) mod 31 gives. Keeping the alphabet as a parameter meant the cipher could be verified against the 26-letter English example printed in the assignment before being trusted on Romanian. The part that took the most code was again not the cipher but the validation of the input, and printing the intermediate codes in the same six-row layout the assignment uses for working the cipher by hand turned out to be the most useful thing in the program: every result in section 3 can be checked column by column against the Tabula Recta without running anything.
