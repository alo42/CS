1. Short theory

The ciphers of the first three laboratory works operate on one letter at a time. A block cipher works instead on fixed-size groups of bits, transforming a whole block under the control of a key, and it is the form in which practically all modern symmetric encryption is done. The Data Encryption Standard, published as FIPS 46 in 1977 and withdrawn in 2005, is the block cipher every later design is measured against: it encrypts 64-bit blocks under a 64-bit key of which only 56 bits are used, in 16 rounds of a Feistel network.

1.1 The structure of the algorithm

A DES encryption of one block runs as follows:

  1. the 64 bits of the block are rearranged by the initial permutation IP and split into two halves of 32 bits, L₀ and R₀;
  2. sixteen rounds are applied. Round i keeps the right half as the new left half and adds the output of the round function to the old left half:
    Li = Ri−1     Ri = Li−1 ⊕ f(Ri−1, Ki)
  3. after round 16 the two halves are swapped once more and the 64 bits R₁₆L₁₆ are rearranged by IP⁻¹, the inverse of the initial permutation, giving the ciphertext.

This shape is called a Feistel network, and it has one property that matters above all others: the round function f need not be invertible. Whatever f does, the round can be undone by knowing Ri−1 = Li and computing Li−1 = Ri ⊕ f(Li, Ki). Decryption is therefore the very same algorithm with the sixteen round keys applied in the reverse order, K₁₆ first — which is why the key schedule is worth implementing on its own, as this task does.

1.2 The round function

The function f(R, K) turns 32 bits and a 48-bit round key into 32 bits in four steps:

  • Expansion. E stretches the 32 bits of R to 48 by repeating 16 of them, so that the block and the round key have the same length.
  • Key mixing. The round key is added bit by bit modulo 2: E(R) ⊕ K. This is the only place the key enters the data path, and it is why the sixteen round keys are the whole of what the key contributes to the cipher.
  • Substitution. The 48 bits are cut into eight groups of six, B₁ … B₈, and each group enters its own S-box, which replaces 6 bits with 4: the outer bits b₁b₆ choose one of four rows, the inner bits b₂b₃b₄b₅ one of sixteen columns. The eight S-boxes are the only non-linear part of DES and the reason it resists the attacks of the previous laboratory works.
  • Permutation. The 32 bits that come out are rearranged by P, which spreads the output of each S-box across the inputs of several S-boxes of the next round — the diffusion of the cipher.

1.3 The key schedule — the subject of this work

The sixteen round keys are produced from the key alone, before any data is touched:

K  —PC-1→  K+  →  C0D0
Ci = LSi(Ci−1)    Di = LSi(Di−1)    Ki = PC-2(CiDi)

PC-1 (Permuted Choice 1) selects 56 of the 64 bits of the key and reorders them, giving K+. The eight bits it leaves out are those in positions 8, 16, 24, …, 64 — the parity bit of each byte, which carries no key material. This is the reason a "64-bit" DES key has an effective length of only 56 bits.

K+ is split into two halves of 28 bits, C₀ and D₀. In round i each half is rotated to the left on its own by LSi places — one place in rounds 1, 2, 9 and 16, and two in the other twelve. The sixteen shifts add up to 28, the length of a half, so after the last round the halves are back where they started: C₁₆ = C₀ and D₁₆ = D₀. That is a useful check on any implementation, and it is also what lets the same schedule be generated in reverse for decryption.

PC-2 (Permuted Choice 2) then selects 48 of the 56 bits of CiDi and reorders them, giving the round key Ki. Eight positions — 9, 18, 22, 25, 35, 38, 43 and 54 — are never named by PC-2 and are therefore dropped in every round; because the halves rotate, a bit dropped in one round is used in the next, so every key bit takes part in about fourteen of the sixteen rounds.

1.4 Security

DES was never broken mathematically in the way the ciphers of laboratory works 1 to 3 were: after more than forty years of analysis, differential and linear cryptanalysis are the best attacks known and both need quantities of chosen or known plaintext that are impractical. What defeated DES was the key length. A 56-bit key allows only 2⁵⁶ ≈ 7.2 × 10¹⁶ possibilities, and exhaustive search became feasible in hardware: the EFF "Deep Crack" machine recovered a key in 56 hours in 1998. The response was Triple DES, which applies the algorithm three times with two or three keys, and then AES, which replaced DES as the standard in 2001.

Bit strings

A block is a plain list of 0/1 integers. FIPS 46-3 numbers the bits of a block from 1 on the left and writes every table in those terms, so the permutation function indexes its table 1-based and nothing in the program has to re-number anything:

def permute(block, table):
    """Return the block rearranged by `table`, a 1-based list of positions."""
    return [block[position - 1] for position in table]


def rotate_left(block, amount):
    """Rotate a block one or more places to the left (the LS_i of the standard)."""
    amount %= len(block)
    return block[amount:] + block[:amount]

One function therefore performs both PC-1 and PC-2 — and, in the data path, IP, E, P and IP⁻¹ as well, since in DES every table is read the same way: the bit of the output at position i is the bit of the input at position table[i]. That single observation is what keeps the implementation short.

The tables

PC-1, PC-2 and the shift schedule are transcribed from the standard. Two facts about them are computed rather than written down, so that a typing mistake in a table would be caught instead of being believed:

PARITY_BITS = tuple(position for position in range(1, KEY_BITS + 1)
                    if position not in PC1)

DROPPED_BY_PC2 = tuple(position for position in range(1, KPLUS_BITS + 1)
                       if position not in PC2)

These produce (8, 16, 24, 32, 40, 48, 56, 64) and (9, 18, 22, 25, 35, 38, 43, 54), which are exactly the positions the standard says are discarded — a first confirmation that the tables were copied correctly.

The key schedule

def permuted_choice_1(key):
    """K+ = PC-1(K): drop the 8 parity bits of the key and reorder the rest."""
    return bits.permute(key, PC1)


def split(kplus):
    """C_0 and D_0: the left and the right half of K+."""
    return kplus[:HALF_BITS], kplus[HALF_BITS:]


def halves(kplus):
    """All the pairs (C_i, D_i), from i = 0 to i = 16."""
    c, d = split(kplus)
    pairs = [(c, d)]
    for shift in SHIFTS:
        c = bits.rotate_left(c, shift)
        d = bits.rotate_left(d, shift)
        pairs.append((c, d))
    return pairs


def round_key(c, d):
    """K_i = PC-2(C_i D_i): 48 of the 56 bits of the two halves."""
    return bits.permute(c + d, PC2)


def round_keys(kplus):
    """The sixteen round keys K_1 ... K_16 - the answer to task 2.5."""
    return [round_key(c, d) for c, d in halves(kplus)[1:]]

A further function, schedule(), returns one record per round holding the shift applied, both halves before and after it and the resulting round key. The cipher itself needs only round_keys(); schedule() exists because the assignment requires every intermediate step to be displayed, and keeping the two apart means the printing code never has to recompute anything.

Input

The program accepts the key as 8 characters — the "8 simboluri" of the assignment, one byte each — or as 16 hexadecimal digits, which is how published DES test keys are written. Since 16 hexadecimal digits are never 8 characters, the two cannot be confused. K+ may be typed directly as 56 bits or 14 hexadecimal digits, and a key can be generated at random. Everything that is rejected is reported with the reason, and the question is asked again.

5. Verification

Every value in section 4 agrees with the standard worked example of DES, and the tests pin all sixteen round keys, not only the first and the last that are usually quoted.

That check has a limit, though: it compares the program against one published example. A round key cannot be verified against anything on its own — it is 48 bits with no structure to inspect. The only independent evidence that the schedule is right is that a complete DES encryption built on these sixteen keys reproduces a known ciphertext, because a single wrong bit anywhere in the schedule changes the output completely. So tests/des_reference.py adds the data path of section 1.2 — IP, E, the eight S-boxes, P and IP⁻¹ — and drives it with the round keys this program produces:

All three are reproduced exactly. That file is a test fixture and is not part of task 2.5; it exists so that the claim "the sixteen round keys are correct" can be checked by running the tests rather than taken on trust.

The remaining tests cover properties that must hold for any K+, not only for the example: that PC-1 and PC-2 name distinct positions in range and discard exactly the eight documented ones, that the shifts add up to 28 and are single only in rounds 1, 2, 9 and 16, that every round key is 48 bits, that the sixteen are all different, that C₁₆ = C₀, that flipping any parity bit of the key leaves all sixteen round keys unchanged, and that flipping a real key bit disturbs most of them.

6. Conclusions

The key schedule is the smallest part of DES and the one that explains the most about it. Three facts fall straight out of implementing it. The first is where the "64-bit key" of DES goes: PC-1 discards eight of those bits before anything else happens, and the test that flips each of them in turn and gets the same sixteen round keys shows directly that they carry nothing. The effective key is 56 bits, and that — not any weakness in the rounds — is what ended DES.

The second is that the schedule is built out of nothing but rotations and two fixed selections. There is no arithmetic in it at all, which is what makes the C₁₆ = C₀ identity possible: the sixteen shifts were chosen to add up to 28 precisely so that the halves return to their starting position, which lets the same hardware generate the keys forwards for encryption and backwards for decryption. It is a design decision visible in a one-line check at the end of the program's output.

The third is how little separates the key schedule from a cipher that does nothing. Because the schedule is linear, a key whose K+ is all zeros produces sixteen identical all-zero round keys — the degenerate case the tests exercise — and the four DES weak keys arise in exactly that way, from halves that are constant under rotation. The strength of DES is not in the key schedule but in the S-boxes, and the key schedule's job is only to make sure every round sees a different 48 bits.

On the implementation side, the single most useful decision was to write permute() once, 1-based, in the same terms the standard uses, and let PC-1 and PC-2 — and later IP, E, P and IP⁻¹ in the verification fixture — all be calls to it. The tables are then the only thing that can be wrong, and deriving the discarded positions from the tables rather than writing them down turns a transcription error into a failing test. The verification was the other lesson: checking the program against a published worked example proves only that it matches that example, while running a full DES encryption on its output and reproducing three known ciphertexts is evidence that does not depend on having copied the reference values correctly in the first place.
